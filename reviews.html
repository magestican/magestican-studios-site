Reviews

Two things live on this page. Underneath, our own assessment of every game we have published — what it is, what works, and what is still wrong with it. At the top, the shelf where reviews written by players will go, which is empty, and which we are not going to fill in ourselves.

What players say

Nothing yet. No player has sent us a review, so there are none on this page. There are no quotes, no names, no stars and no rating average, because we have not been given any.

That is worth saying out loud rather than leaving as an absence. It would take about ten minutes to write five glowing testimonials, attribute them to plausible first names, and put a 4.8 out of 5 under them, and a great many small sites do exactly that. Inventing a customer is not a design decision, it is a false statement about a real company, and it is the sort of thing that is only ever discovered at the worst possible moment.

So this section stays empty until it is not. When real reviews arrive they will appear here with the name the writer gave, the date, and the game they played — unedited apart from length, and marked where they have been cut. Critical ones will be published alongside the kind ones; a page of nothing but praise is a page nobody believes.

The games are free and there is nothing to buy, so there is nothing in it for us either way except knowing whether they are any good. If you have played one, we would like to hear it — the studio's contact channels are on the about page.

Farmyshoot

Playable now 3D voxel shooter · up to 16 players · browser, phone or desktop

What it is

A first-person shooter that runs in a browser tab with no download, no installer and no account. You are a cow, a chicken, a pig or a sheep, on a snow map, in one of four modes: capture the flag, team deathmatch, king of the hill, and a one-flag variant. Matches are up to sixteen players and bots fill any seat nobody takes. Movement has deliberate ice-drift to it, so you slide when you stop.

The multiplayer is peer-to-peer over WebRTC. There is no game server anywhere: you send somebody a link and the two browsers talk to each other. That is the reason it works on a locked-down work laptop, and it is also the reason there is no matchmaking, no ranked ladder and no persistent profile.

What works

  • The first thirty seconds. Click a link, pick an animal, and you are playing. No launcher, no patch, no sign-in. That is a genuinely uncommon experience in 2026 and it is the best thing about the game.
  • It runs on a phone. Touch controls, and the same link works from a desktop or a handset in the same match.
  • The bots are real opponents. They path, they take cover, they contest the hill and they keep out of each other's way. You will mostly be playing against them, which is the honest situation for a game this new, and it is not a wasted evening.

What is wrong with it

  • Firefox and Safari are fixed but unverified. A single line of syntax made the mouse fail to capture in every version of Firefox ever released, and in Safari older than 18.4. It was found and fixed, and the fix is covered by tests that run the exact production expression against each browser's contract — but the only browser available on the machine this is built on is Chromium, so nobody has opened a real Firefox and played a round. That is a weaker claim than it sounds and we would rather say so.
  • You will usually be alone. There is no player base to speak of and no lobby browser. If you turn up without a friend and a link, you are playing against bots. That is a real limitation of the game as it exists, not a phase we are describing optimistically.
  • Peer-to-peer is not free of network trouble. Some networks will not let two browsers connect directly. There are relays behind it and a failed join now says so out loud rather than hanging, which is an improvement on where it was, but "no server" does mean the connection is more exposed to your network than a normal game's would be.
  • Nothing persists off this browser. Without an account the leaderboard is your own browser's record. Clear your site data and it is gone.
Our verdict

The strongest thing we have made, and the one whose central idea — a real multiplayer shooter that needs nothing installed — is worth the constraints it costs. Play it with somebody. Playing it alone is fine and is not the point of it.

Farmy Kart

Playable now 3D kart racer · six circuits in two cups · browser, phone or desktop

What it is

The Farmyshoot farm, in karts. Six circuits in two cups — Sunflower Circuit, Muddy Bottom and Frostfield Loop on the farm, then Harvest Millrace, Saltmarsh Run and Copperhead Canyon past the fence — with eight-kart grids, drift boosts you charge by holding a slide through a corner, and items including a cowpat and a runaway tractor. Each circuit has a cliff drop where the kart deploys a wing and glides. You race friends by sending a link, peer-to-peer with no server, and bots take any seat nobody claimed. Three difficulty settings, from Sunday Drivers to Stampede.

What works

  • The drift. The nose of the kart and its direction of travel are allowed to disagree, which is the whole feel of the genre, and charging a mini-turbo out of a long bend does what you want it to.
  • The bots argue with each other. Each has a fixed personal appetite for a gamble, so the field genuinely splits at a shortcut mouth rather than filing through in a line. That took some fixing and it reads correctly now.
  • The circuits have real elevation. Not decoration: you drive a rim and a chasm floor thirty metres below it in the same lap.

What is wrong with it

  • It moves weekly. The original three circuits were re-authored wider and faster on 29 August 2026 and every lap time changed by seconds; three more circuits and four more drivers have landed since. If you played it a fortnight ago you have not played the current game, and if you play it in a fortnight the same may be true again.
  • One circuit went backwards in that pass. Grading embankments onto the raised roads left Muddy Bottom with measurably more time spent off the tarmac than before, not less. It is a known regression, it is written down as one, and it is not fixed.
  • The progression is thin, and deliberately so. There are two cups and the second unlocks by winning the first, which is the whole of it. You pick a circuit and you race it; there is no season, no championship and no garage to fill.
  • It has had far less play than it needs. Farmyshoot has been shaken out by real matches; this has mostly been measured by a test harness driving it. Measurement is not the same as play, and we expect the difference to show.
Our verdict

The most fun per minute of anything on this site, and the least finished. Worth twenty minutes today; not yet worth telling people it is a complete game.

Zelakas In Space

Playable now 2D arcade shooter · single player · keyboard, desktop

What it is

A 2D arcade shooter made in 2014 in ActionScript 3, with Gonzalo and Christian. You dodge asteroids, blackholes and drones, and there are three endings depending on how much of the level you clear. The original Flash build is playable today in a modern browser through Ruffle, an emulator that runs the original file rather than a port of it.

What works

  • It is the actual 2014 game. Not a remake, not a reconstruction from memory — the file that shipped, running. That is the interesting thing about it.
  • Three endings is a real structure for a game this size, and it gives a reason to go back once.

What is wrong with it

  • It plays like a student game from 2014, because it is one. We are fond of it. That is not the same as recommending it on its merits against anything made this decade.
  • It runs on an emulator, so it is exactly as accurate as that emulator is. Anything that looks off may be the emulation rather than the game, and we cannot always tell which.
  • Keyboard only. It does not work on a phone, and there is no touch layer planned for the original build.
  • The original file is large, so the first load is slower than everything else on this site.
Our verdict

Worth ten minutes as a piece of preserved work, and it is kept online for that reason. A rebuild is intended; until then, judge it as an archive rather than as a release.

2D Fighter EX

Playable now Animation experiment · not a game

What it is

An animation whose project file was lost and which was recovered frame by frame from a screen recording: 84 frames of timing, a camera that pushes in to twice its size, and where each fighter stood. That timing is now replayed with 1990s cel-anime characters, on a forest stage generated from a seed. You can scrub it, slow it down, and reseed the stage.

What works

  • The recovery itself, which is the point. The timing of the original exchange survives, taken back out of a video after the source was gone.
  • Being able to slow it down is the right control to give somebody, because the interest is entirely in the frame timing.

What is wrong with it

  • It is not a game and should not be called one. You do not control a fighter, you cannot win and there is no opponent. It is on the site because it is interesting, not because it is playable in the sense the other three are.
  • It will disappoint anybody who arrives expecting a fighting game. The name does not help. EX is short for experiment.
Our verdict

Five minutes, once, if the idea of pulling animation timing back out of a screen recording appeals to you. If it does not, skip it — there is nothing else in there.

Farmy Crosswords

Playable now Four word games · 223 puzzles · built for older eyes

What it is

Wordle, Spelling Bee, Connections and Strands on one page, with a farm flavour and no clock anywhere. Sixty-five Wordle words, fifty-three hives, fifty-two Connections sets and fifty-three Strands grids, all reachable from a dropdown rather than doled out one a day. The look is a printed almanac — cream paper, heavy ink outlines, five flat colours — and it was specified in writing before any of it was built, because the audience it is for is one that most puzzle games design past rather than for.

What works

  • It is genuinely readable. Nothing is under 18 pixels, nothing pressable is under 48 by 48, and text clears AAA contrast rather than AA. That is a different game to play in poor light than the ones it is modelled on.
  • No state is signalled by colour alone. Every tile carries a shape in its corner and a spoken label as well as a fill, so a green-gold-grey row still reads if you cannot separate those hues.
  • The Strands grids are properly tiled. Every one of the 48 cells belongs to exactly one theme word, which is what lets you finish the last word by elimination. They were packed by a program and each is checked by the same validator the deploy gate runs.
  • Nothing is locked. There is a puzzle of the day, and there is also a list of all 223.

What is wrong with it

  • The Wordle guess list is common words, not a full dictionary. About seventeen hundred five-letter words. A real word you type can be refused, and the message says so plainly rather than pretending otherwise — but it is still a refusal, and it will happen to somebody.
  • The Spelling Bee only accepts the words on its own list. Each hive carries an authored answer list, so a legitimate word the setter did not think of is not accepted. That is a deliberate trade — the alternative is a shared dictionary that is wrong in a more confusing way — but it is a limit, not a feature.
  • Strands is tap-to-select, not drag. Deliberate, because a drag held across eight cells is exactly the input a tremor or a trackpad is worst at — but if you know the original, this will feel unfamiliar for the first minute.
  • The puzzles are ours, and they are a first set. The Connections sets in particular are the part most likely to have a group somebody can argue with. If you find one, that is a defect and we would like to hear about it.
  • There is no sound at all. A choice rather than an omission, and some people will find it flat.
Our verdict

The one on this site to send to a parent. It does what the games it is modelled on do, without the clock, the lockout or the small grey type — and if you want the originals' word lists rather than ours, you will notice the difference.

Why there are no scores on this page

No stars, no marks out of ten, no average. Not an oversight, and worth explaining because their absence is conspicuous.

A rating is a comparison, and it only carries information if the person giving it could plausibly have given a low one. We cannot. Any number we put next to our own game would be a number we chose to publish about ourselves, and everybody reading it knows that, so it would tell you nothing you did not already know and would make the honest paragraphs above look like set dressing for it.

There is a second, more practical reason. Search engines show star ratings in their results, and they specifically exclude self-authored reviews from that treatment. Marking up our own assessment as a review with a rating would be asking for stars we are not entitled to, and it would put this page's standing at risk to decorate it. So this page carries no review markup and no aggregate rating — only a plain description of what it is.

If a player review arrives that carries a rating, that rating is theirs and it will be shown as theirs, including if it is low.

What is missing from this page that we would like to add: comparisons against other games in each genre, and figures on how long a session actually lasts. Both need players. Neither is going to be invented in the meantime.
