Three ways to guess where a ramp is, and why we stopped guessing

28 August 2026 Farmy Kart 7 min read

A kart racer needs jumps. The tracks already had hills, so it seemed obvious that a jump should just be what happens when you drive fast over one. Three models were built to detect that. All three failed, and the third failed for the most interesting reason.

Farmy Kart is a browser kart racer with three circuits and eight karts on the grid. When the tracks got real elevation — rolling ground, a valley, embankments where the road is raised above the field — the next ask was for jumps.

There is an elegant version of that feature and an unglamorous one. The elegant version is that a jump is not a thing you place; it is a thing that happens. Drive fast enough over a crest and the ground falls away from under you and you are in the air, and nobody had to author anything. The unglamorous version is a marker in the track data that says throw the kart here, this hard.

The elegant version was tried three times.

One: the ground is rising fast

The first model is the one anybody would write. Sample the ground height under the kart each frame, differentiate it, and if it is climbing quickly, that is a ramp.

It launched karts seven times a lap on Muddy Bottom, a circuit that has no jump on it at all and is simply a valley.

The cause is in how the ground height is obtained. It is sampled from the nearest point on a spline — the smooth curve the road is built along — and "the nearest point" is not a continuous function. It steps. At a hairpin, at the mouth of a shortcut, or on any frame a respawn moves a kart, the nearest segment changes and the sampled height jumps to a different part of the curve.

A 20 cm step in a single frame reads as a rise of 12 metres per second. The detector was not measuring the terrain. It was measuring its own bookkeeping.

Two: require the rise to be sustained

If single-frame steps are the problem, ignore single frames. Demand that the ground has been climbing for a while before calling it a ramp.

That removed every false positive and every real one with it, and the reason is a property of the curve rather than of the code. The spline is centripetal, which means it rounds off corners rather than driving into them, and rounding a crest puts the steepest part of a ramp halfway up its face — not at the lip. By the time the kart reaches the top, the slope is already easing.

So the kart took off two-thirds of the way up the ramp, hopped a metre, and came down on the ramp again. Which is worse than no jump, because it looks like a bug in the suspension.

Three: the physically correct one

There is a right answer to "when does a vehicle leave the ground", and it is not about the slope at all. You leave the ground when the surface curves away from under you faster than gravity can pull you down onto it — when your speed squared over the radius of the curve exceeds g. That is the real condition. It is what a car does on a humpback bridge.

It is also unusable here, and it is unusable for the same reason the other two were, one derivative deeper. Curvature is the second derivative of the height, and the height is a noisy sampled signal. Differentiating noise once gives you loud noise. Differentiating it twice gives you nothing else.

Measured: it fired 137 times in three laps. Every one of them was a two-metre hop.

What the three failures have in common

A jump is a property of the curve’s sharp features, and sampling is exactly the operation that destroys sharp features.

That sentence is the whole finding, and it is why no fourth attempt was made. The ground height available to the physics is an approximation of a smooth curve, taken at points. The information that says "there is a lip right here" lives in between those points. No amount of filtering recovers something that was never sampled.

So a jump is now a thing a track says it has: a marker at a lap fraction, with a launch strength and a minimum speed. That is also what every kart racer in the genre does, which should have been a hint. It is deterministic, it is tunable per circuit, and — the part that mattered most — it can be tested, because it is a decision taken once rather than a guess re-made sixty times a second.

The terrain still does all the visual work. The marker sits on a lip that really is a lip, so the launch and the ramp agree. What the declaration adds is not the shape; it is the certainty.

The first ramp made the bots brake

Authoring the first one produced a defect that had nothing to do with jumping. The bots were braking to 95 km/h on the longest straight on the roster, and arriving at the ramp too slowly to take off.

Building the ramp meant adding control points to the spline to shape its face, and they were placed 20 metres apart on a run that is otherwise nearly straight. Bunching points like that puts real curvature into the curve between them, and the racing line reads curvature as a corner. The bots were braking for a bend that existed only in the data.

The ramp points are now exactly collinear in plan and change only their height, so the curve stays straight while the ground goes up.

The honest footnote: the authoring notes at the top of that same file warned about bunching control points. I did it anyway, and then measured my way back to the warning I had already written.

The part that makes it worth writing down

A day later the tracks got cliffs, and with them a glider — drive off a thirty-metre drop and a wing deploys. The obvious lesson from the ramps is that a glide must be declared too, for the same reason.

It is the wrong lesson, and seeing why is the useful bit. A ramp is a property of the ground’s curvature, which is the thing sampling destroys. A glide is a property of the ground’s distance — how far below you it is — and distance survives sampling perfectly. It is not even a new measurement: the height above ground is a number the physics already computes every frame, to decide whether the kart has landed. It is the same number, read one line earlier.

There is no derivative anywhere in the glider. Which means it works on any long drop, including one authored later, on a circuit that declares nothing.

Two features that look like the same problem, and the right answer is opposite for each. The difference is not the terrain. It is which question you are asking of the same signal.
