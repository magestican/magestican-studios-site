The hash that was a lattice, and the shortcut nobody could take

28 August 2026 Farmy Kart 6 min read

One of Farmy Kart’s shortcuts had never been taken by a computer-controlled driver, on any grid, at any difficulty. It was not badly tuned. It was arithmetically impossible, and the reason was one line that looked like a hash and was not.

An uncontested shortcut is a corridor, not a decision.

That is why this work happened at all. Farmy Kart’s circuits have branch lines — a cut through the sunflowers, a frozen creek, a cattle gate — and if only the human player ever takes them, they are not shortcuts. Nobody is alongside you in there, nobody beats you to the mouth, and the field you rejoin has not moved. The gamble has no other side.

So the eight computer-controlled drivers needed to be able to take them, and to disagree about whether to, because a field where everybody makes the same choice is one decision, not eight.

Nerve

Each driver gets a personal appetite for a gamble, called nerve. It is fixed at creation from the driver’s slot on the grid rather than rolled fresh, for two reasons: a race replayed from the same seed has to be the same race, and a wobble re-rolled every frame is noise rather than character.

Deriving a number in the range nought to one from a small integer looks like a solved problem, and the line that did it was this shape:

(seedIndex * bigPrime) % 1000 / 1000

It is a common idiom, it looks like scrambling, and for eight consecutive inputs it is not a hash at all. Multiplying by a constant and taking a modulus is an arithmetic sequence by construction. The multiply moves the values a long way; it does not mix them. Measured across the eight grid slots:

0.000   0.519   0.038   0.557   0.076   0.595   0.114   0.633

Read the odd and even positions separately and you can see it: two interleaved arithmetic sequences, each stepping by about 0.038. And a ceiling — across the whole grid, nerve never once exceeded 0.64.

The consequence

Muddy Bottom’s cattle gate is the meanest cut on the roster: low grip, narrow, and it costs you if you get it wrong. At Market Day pace it needs a nerve above 0.66.

Nothing on the grid could produce a 0.66. Not an unlikely event — an unreachable one. No driver in any race that had ever been run could have taken that shortcut, and none ever did.

What makes this worth an article is not the bug. It is that from the outside it was invisible in the most expensive possible way: it looked like a tuning problem. The gate is hard, bots do not take it, so the threshold must be a bit high — and every hour spent adjusting that threshold was an hour spent on a number that was never going to be compared against anything above 0.64.

The nerve value is now produced by a murmur3 finalizer: shift, multiply, exclusive-or, three times over. That is what actually avalanches small consecutive inputs across the range.

What was deliberately not fixed

Two other values on the same driver — its line bias and its steering phase — are computed with exactly the same broken idiom, and they were left alone.

They need variety, not a distribution. Nothing compares them against a threshold; they only have to differ from one another so the field does not drive as a single organism, and a lattice differs from itself perfectly well. Changing them would move every bot’s line on every circuit and invalidate every lap time measured to date, in exchange for nothing anyone could see.

Fixing the two harmless instances of a bug you have just understood is very tempting and is a real cost.

Two more, found on the way

Neither announced itself.

The skill lookup read fields that do not exist. It asked for difficulty.aim ?? difficulty.skill. A difficulty entry has neither; it has precision and driftSkill. So every driver on every setting fell through to the same fallback constant, and all three difficulties made identical choices — eight of eight drivers took every shortcut on every difficulty. A ?? chain onto a misremembered field name fails silently and plausibly: the code reads as considered, and the wrong answer it produces is a legal one.

The commitment flapped. A driver decides about a branch while still on the main road, and therefore a long way from the branch line. A test for "have I strayed off the line I committed to", applied immediately, released the commitment on the very first frame — and because releasing also cleared the decision, the driver re-committed on the next frame, and so on. Measured: 680 commitments across fifteen laps of a circuit with one shortcut on it.

The result

With drivers that can take the cuts and disagree about them, the field splits at the branch mouth, and lap times moved:

Sunflower Fields   25.9 -> 23.9 s   faster by 1.97 s/lap
Frostfield         24.3 -> 21.9 s   faster by 2.39 s/lap
Muddy Bottom       22.8 -> 21.2 s   faster by 1.57 s/lap

Two seconds a lap is a large number in a race that lasts about ninety. But the number that mattered was 0.64, and it took measuring the distribution to see it — because everything downstream of a broken hash still runs, still looks reasonable, and still produces a race.
